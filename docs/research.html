Field Experience in Ghana

January 2026 – May 2026 | Ghana Visit: March 19–29, 2026

From Dome to the Mall: What Accra’s Food Markets Reveal About Trade-offs, Tech, & Tomorrow

This research was conducted as part of a field experience program in Ghana, funded by the Rodger P. Kennedy and Mariette B. Kennedy Fund, with guidance from Professor Claudena Skran.

Research Questions:

  • What truly shapes food access in Accra beyond simple market connectivity?
  • How do informal open markets and modern retail differ in pricing, supply, and livelihoods?
  • Can Agri-tech meaningfully close the gaps in Accra’s food system?

Overview:

Food access in Accra is shaped by trade-offs between affordability, flexibility, and convenience across two simultaneously operating food systems — vibrant informal open markets and expanding modern retail — each serving different economic realities. Ghana’s informal sector employs over 80% of the national workforce and dominates food retail for most urban households.

Through in-depth interviews with sellers and consumers at Dome Market and Accra Mall (roughly 7.8 miles apart but worlds apart economically), field observations, and desk research on Ghana’s informal economy and agricultural technology, this study uncovered a core finding: market access is not the problem — perishability and price volatility are. The complete absence of cold storage means tomatoes spoil within 3–4 days, losses are silently absorbed, and vendor livelihoods remain precarious.

Modern retail solves spoilage through cold chain infrastructure and warehouse-backed supply, but creates a new problem: price exclusion. When formal retail imports what local farmers grow — sourcing produce from South Africa even during Ghana’s own harvest seasons — it disconnects entirely from the local food economy.

Key Findings:

  • Informal markets offer flexibility, negotiated pricing, and strong community trust, but zero cold storage leads to massive post-harvest losses
  • Ghana loses approximately 40% of its 420,000 MT annual tomato production to spoilage — over $31 million lost in Agbogbloshie alone
  • Modern retail solves perishability but prices out the majority of Accra’s population
  • Low-cost solar-powered cold storage at the market level is the most viable near-term intervention, with projections of over $73,000 net profit in year one at a single market

Conclusion:

Ghana’s food system challenge is not a production problem — it is a system design and coordination problem. Lasting change requires investment in agri-tech solutions, policy support, and placing informal vendors at the center of the solution rather than the margins.

LURF Student Research: Business Ethnography Curriculum Design

June 2025 – September 2025

Designing a Course in Business Ethnography at Lawrence University

Collaborators: Tahana Akram ’27, Faith Onukaogu ’26, Prof. Carla Daughtry Departments: Business and Entrepreneurship, Anthropology — Lawrence University

Note: This course is now being offered at Lawrence University as BUEN 240.

Overview:

This Lawrence University Research Fellowship (LURF) project involved designing a full undergraduate course curriculum in Business Ethnography — a field that bridges anthropological research methods with real-world business applications. The goal was to create a rigorous, interdisciplinary course that equips students to understand the behaviors, values, and decision-making of people within business contexts using tools like participant observation, in-depth interviews, and cultural analysis.

What the Course Covers:

Business Ethnography examines how social, emotional, and cultural factors shape consumer behavior, organizational culture, and product development. The curriculum was designed around three guiding questions:

  • How can ethnographic methods address real-world business challenges while maintaining ethical integrity?
  • In what ways do cultural values and norms shape consumer behavior and influence marketing strategies?
  • What role does business ethnography play in promoting diversity, equity, and inclusion in global workplaces?

Curriculum Structure:

The course runs 10 weeks, moving from foundational methods through applied industry contexts:

  • Weeks 1–2: Introduction to Business Ethnography & Core Ethnographic Methods (participant observation, fieldnotes, reflexivity, in-depth interviews)
  • Weeks 3–4: Organizational Culture & the Role of Ethnography in Marketing and Product Development
  • Weeks 5–6: Ethics, Corporate Social Responsibility, and Consumer Culture
  • Weeks 7–8: Design Anthropology & Industry-Specific Ethnography
  • Weeks 9–10: Global Business & Future Directions in Technology

Final Project:

Students produce a 10–15 minute podcast episode applying course concepts and ethnographic methods to a real-world business issue, integrating at least three course readings and original interviews or field observations. A 500–700 word written summary accompanies the audio submission.

What We Found:

Designing this curriculum revealed a significant gap in business education — most programs teach market research through quantitative lenses alone, overlooking the rich qualitative and cultural insights that ethnographic methods provide. By combining anthropology and business, this course gives students tools to ask why people behave the way they do, not just what they do — a distinction that matters enormously in marketing, innovation, and organizational design.
